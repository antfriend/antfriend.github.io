Personal Grimoire · release notes

What came of it

The Personal Grimoire keeps one person's words in a single file and answers from them, quoting what was said. The grammar it reads them with is in the same file. Over seven phases and a series of RFCs, it learned to read constructions, parts of clauses, two languages at once, the shape a belief's sayings make over time, and how fast what you say of a thing changes. This page shows the best of that. Each section has a small example store, so you can ask the question yourself.

1The file is the grammar

The runtime, index.html, contains no word of any language. Every word it matches, every ending it strips and every phrase it answers with is read from the store, personal_grimoire_ttdb.md, the same file that holds your sayings. A test fails if a word of either grammar appears in the runtime as a string.

The strongest evidence is bAbI [1]. Apply one fixed letter substitution to the grammar records and to the task data, so that John becomes something like Qrzh, and the answers are the same, question for question, with the same outcome hash. Apply it to the grammar alone and the grimoire understands nothing. Its English is in the file, not in the code.

The four example stores below are built the same way: the seed store's grammar, emptied of its sayings, with a few new ones told. Nothing else changes, and opening one never touches the store you keep in your browser.

Provenance. The design is the project's own: TTDB, the Toot Toot Grammar RFCs and a runtime without words (TTG-RFC-0001). bAbI is Weston et al. [1]; the results are in Measured.

2Constructions, and what they inherit

In Pixel sneezed the napkin off the table, the verb sneeze moves nothing. The napkin goes off the table because of the sentence's shape, not its verb. Goldberg calls this the caused-motion construction [2]: a pattern of form with a meaning of its own. The grimoire declares it in the store, as a line of grammar data:

construction: [X] {V} [Y] off [Z] => go_off Y Z | caused motion | is_a acting on a thing

So the sentence says two things: pixel sneeze napkin and napkin go_off table, and the reply names the construction that read it.

Constructions form a network of default inheritance, as construction grammar describes. A child says what its parents say and adds to it or overrides it. A construction can have several parents. An abstract one is never matched, only inherited. A slot can be tied to particular words, so knock gets a construction of its own, and took the day off is an idiom [3] whose meaning is resting, not taking.

Try it in the constructions store
Where did the napkin go? Where did the cup go? Did I rest the day? What left the bed?

Where did the cup go? answers shelf and floor, each quoting the one sentence that said it. What left the bed? answers ball, from The dog pushed the ball from the bed: after a verb that moves a thing, from means leaving. After bought it doesn't.

Provenance. The idea is Construction Grammar's: caused motion and default inheritance from Goldberg [2], idioms from Fillmore, Kay and O'Connor [3]. The spec is TTG-RFC-0006, from 0.1 (4795447) through inheritance, several parents, abstract roots and tied slots (0.2–0.6, 13b9114–4305092) to moving from (0.16, 9495529).

3Questions a shape answers

A construction reads questions as well as statements. What did Pixel sneeze off the table? is matched against the shape X V Y off Z with one slot left open, and is answered by whatever fills every percept the shape names: something Pixel sneezed and something that went off the table. So the answer is the napkin, not something else Pixel sneezed and not something else that fell. A question can leave the verb open too: What did Pixel do? lists the doings, each with its sentence.

> What did Pixel sneeze off the table?
napkin: [you said] napkin go_off table — “Pixel sneezed the napkin off the table.”
Try it in the constructions store
What did Pixel sneeze off the table? What did Pixel do? Did the napkin leave the table?

The last one is answered probably. Nobody said the napkin left the table. A rule in the store (go_off X Y => leave X Y) infers it, and the reply prints it as inferred, never as said.

Provenance. The same construction-grammar sources as above. The spec is TTG-RFC-0006 §7 and TTG-RFC-0003 §4, from 0.8 (28b9f86) to a gap at the verb and what a subject inherits (0.9–0.11, e8f4367–73289dc). Rules and their inferred grounds are TTG-RFC-0003 §3.1, the project's own.

4Reading part of a clause

A sentence is rarely one clause. In When Pixel knocked the vase off the table, the dog barked, a construction should read the first clause and leave the second to the words. So the grimoire first finds the span each clause covers, then lets a construction read just that span.

A clause under a stance is held: kept and quoted, never believed. I doubt cats like fish makes the grimoire neither believe nor deny that cats like fish. If you ask, it tells you what you said and how.

> Do cats like fish?
Your words don't reach that yet.
You said it, but not as fact: “I doubt cats like fish.”
Try it in the clauses store
What do I doubt? Do cats like fish? Did the cat bite the dog? Where did the vase go? Did the dog bark?
Provenance. A clause a stance holds, linked back to it, is LFG's COMP [4], kept flat. Early I think as a stance marker rather than a main verb is Diessel and Tomasello's finding [5] (Phase 1, 226bf8d). Spans inside a clause are TTG-RFC-0006 0.13–0.14 (68dd995, ac287d0) and 0.17 with TTG-RFC-0002 1.7 (94f1c15).

5Two languages, one motion

The store holds a Spanish grammar beside the English one, and each sentence is read by the grammar it is written in. Spanish de means of far more often than off, so it can't trigger motion on its own. It does after a verb tied to motion: Pixel tiró el vaso de la mesa (Pixel knocked the glass off the table) is read by movimiento causado. Two rules then give the English and Spanish motions one meaning:

rule: go_off X Y => leave X Y | going off a thing is leaving it
rule: salir_de X Y => leave X Y | salir de is leave

So a question in either language reaches a saying in either. Did the vaso leave the mesa? is answered from the Spanish sentence, through the rule, and the reply says which rule it used.

Try it in the two-language store
¿De dónde tiró Pixel el vaso? ¿Qué salió de la mesa? Did the vaso leave the mesa? What left the table?
Provenance. Per-language patterns over one engine echo Principles and Parameters [6], whose parameters the store names in a table (Phase 5, 3b0e72e). The Spanish construction is TTG-RFC-0006 0.7 (c4c5628), the Spanish questions 0.12 (26b5ce4), and the one motion TTG-RFC-0003 1.13 (87b4a45).

6Belief trajectories

A belief's confidence is a sum over its sayings. Three times I like tea and three times I don't like tea is a contest at exactly even, whatever the order. But order matters to a reader. Three and then three looks like a change of mind, while back and forth looks like a real disagreement. The grimoire refuses to guess which saying is current (that would be a claim about you), so the verdict stays a contest. The reply now adds what the order shows, as a note beside the verdict that never changes it.

> Do I like tea?
Your words disagree, and neither is chosen.
You said it one way in 3 episodes, then the other way in the 3 episodes since — perhaps a change of mind. Both still count, so the answer stands.

Where your tense already marks the change (I liked cake, then I don't like cake), the store already treats it as a change rather than a contradiction, and the existing note stands alone.

Try it in the trajectories store
Do I like tea? Do I like milk? Do I like jam? Do I like cake?

The re-reading tool also counts how many sentences each construction reads under today's grammar. Run it before and after a grammar change to see which constructions gained sentences and which lost them: node tools/reread.mjs; for every triple's shape, node tools/trajectories.mjs.

Provenance. The idea came from Haylett's reading of language as a nonlinear dynamical system [7], which rebuilds an attractor from one series with Takens' delay embedding [8]. The grimoire borrows the picture, not the theorem: a triple's series is short and discrete. The spec is TTG-RFC-0003 1.15 §4 and its Appendix A, TTG-RFC-0001 1.11 and TTG-RFC-0005 1.6 §5.1 (2bef5e2).

7The slow verb: a meter for how fast your words change

The grimoire splits what you say into things and vectors, nouns and verbs, and the split is the first line on its sphere. Haylett's “Geofinitism and the Slow Verb” [12] argues that the split is a matter of how long you watch: the noun is the slow verb. A stone counts as a noun because it weathers more slowly than anyone looks, and a cat moving from room to room changes fast enough to count as a verb. The store has a measuring window of its own, its episodes, and now it measures in it.

The pace of a term is how many changes there are in what you say of it, per episode that says anything of it. A change is a turn, where one of its triples switches sign between episodes (the runs from section 6), or a move, where along an exclusive vector a later saying puts it somewhere else. A term with no changes is still, one under pace_fast is slow, and one at or over it is fast.

> Tell me about Pixel.
What your words hold about pixel.
Fast enough to see: what you say of pixel has turned 3 times in 4 episodes — in your words it moves like a verb.

The meter is shown in three places: as a note when you ask Tell me about …, as a bar on a term's panel, and as a pace sort on the term list, fastest first. It is a reading of the corpus and decides nothing, so a fast term's beliefs count exactly as a still one's do. It does not yet see a place reached by a rule (moved to the kitchen), and it does not count a meaning that drifts only by gaining objects, because that is accumulation rather than a turn.

Try it in the slow-verb store
Tell me about the stone. Tell me about the computer. Tell me about the cloud. Tell me about Pixel. Is a computer a person?

For every term's pace, fastest first: node tools/pace.mjs (add --store examples/slow_verb_ttdb.md for this store).

Provenance. The idea is Haylett's [12]: a word's category depends on the measuring window through which its referent is sampled. The grimoire takes the window to be the episode, and a change to be what the store can already show, a turn or a move. The design belief is @LAT98LON10. It is a prototype, and its spec has not yet been written upstream.

8Where it sits among theories of grammar

Before the phases began, the grimoire was compared, dimension by dimension, with four theories of grammar. Each phase then took one idea from them that the grimoire could hold without giving up quoting its owner.

TheoryWhat the grimoire sharesWhat it doesn't
Lexical-Functional Grammar [4]a surface structure beside a function structure, read without transformations; flat stand-ins for COMP, TENSE and ADJUNCTnested f-structures
Principles & Parameters [6]one universal engine and per-language parameters, now a named tablederivations, and the autonomy of syntax that constructions give up
Usage-based grammar [9] [10]exemplar memory, verb-by-verb learning, one individual speaker; verb frames counted in episodes, optionally preemptingentrenchment by frequency, unless its numbers ask for it
Construction Grammar [2]declared form–meaning pairs, default and multiple inheritance, abstract schemas, verb-specific constructions and idiomscoercing the verb itself; slots tied to meanings rather than words

None of the four has anything like the grimoire's provenance: every answer quotes the sentences it came from, inferred grounds are never printed as said ones, and contradictions are kept rather than resolved.

9Measured

bAbI v1.2 English test splits, 1,000 questions each [1]. Each story's facts are told to an emptied store, one episode per line. Exact attribution means the answer is right and the sentences it quotes are exactly the gold supporting facts. The runs were repeated after each phase, after the constructions series and after Phase 7. Each time the outcome hashes equalled the ones recorded in paper/, so none of this work changed a bAbI answer.

The permuted rows are the claim from section 1. With the grammar and data enciphered alike, the answers are identical. With the grammar enciphered alone, nothing is understood. Removing exclusive, the flag that lets a later place retire an earlier one, drops task 1 to about half.

10Where the ideas came from

Three kinds of origin, kept apart:

Ideas from the literature

Cited where they are used, and listed below. In short: LFG's COMP, TENSE and ADJUNCT [4] for Phases 1, 3 and 4; Principles and Parameters [6] for Phase 5; usage-based entrenchment and preemption [9] [10] for Phase 2, and I think as a stance marker [5]; Construction Grammar [2] and idioms [3] for Phase 6; Laplace's rule of succession [11] for a belief's confidence; bAbI [1] for the benchmark; Haylett [7] and Takens [8] for trajectories, and Haylett again [12] for the slow-verb meter. Each idea was cut down to what a store can hold without claiming anything about its owner that the owner didn't say.

The project's own design

TTDB (the file format, its globe of records and its typed edges), the Toot Toot Grammar RFCs, the runtime that holds no word, and the store that is its own grammar are by the project's author, antfriend, and specified in the RFCs. So are the grimoire's quoting guarantees: said and inferred never printed alike, contradictions kept, amendments beside the episodes rather than over them.

How it was built

The comparison with the four theories, Phases 1–9 and the TTG-RFC-0006 series were worked out in sessions with an AI coding assistant, Claude Code, as the Co-Authored-By lines on the commits record. Every spec change is a commit in toot-toot-engineering, and each RFC changelog entry since 1.0 names the runtime commit that implemented it, so each claim on this page traces to spec text and code.
